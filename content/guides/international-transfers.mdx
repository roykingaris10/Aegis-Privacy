# International Transfers Post-Adequacy

International data transfers sit at the intersection of law, geopolitics, and operational reality. For UK-based controllers and processors, the rules in <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Chapter V" /> set the baseline: personal data may only leave the UK where the destination provides an adequate level of protection, or where an appropriate safeguard is in place. This guide walks through the current adequacy landscape, the available transfer mechanisms, and the practical steps you need to take to transfer personal data lawfully.

## The Adequacy Landscape

Adequacy decisions are the simplest route for international transfers. When the Secretary of State determines that a third country ensures an adequate level of protection for personal data, controllers can transfer data to that country without needing additional safeguards.

### UK Adequacy Decisions

Following Brexit, the UK carried over the EU's existing adequacy decisions and began making its own independent assessments. The UK currently recognises adequacy for a number of jurisdictions, including the EEA/EU member states, Switzerland, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Canada (commercial organisations), Israel, Argentina, Uruguay, and others. The full list is maintained by the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT) and the ICO.

<LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 45">Transfers on the basis of an adequacy decision</LegalCite>

### The EU-UK Relationship

The European Commission granted the UK an adequacy decision in June 2021, enabling personal data to flow freely from the EU/EEA into the UK. That decision included a built-in sunset clause of four years, meaning it was due to expire in June 2025. The Commission renewed the adequacy finding, but practitioners should remain alert: adequacy is never permanent. Changes to UK domestic surveillance law, the evolving shape of the UK's own data protection reform programme, and political shifts could all trigger a review.

From the UK side, adequacy for the EEA is reciprocal. The UK recognises EU/EEA member states as adequate, so transfers from the UK to the EU do not require additional safeguards.

<Callout type="warning" title="Adequacy Is Not Forever">
Adequacy decisions can be revoked, amended, or allowed to lapse. Build your transfer architecture so that you can fall back to alternative mechanisms -- such as the IDTA or SCCs -- at short notice if an adequacy decision is withdrawn.
</Callout>

### Monitoring and Renewal

Under <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 45(4)">Ongoing monitoring of adequacy</LegalCite>, the Secretary of State must keep adequacy decisions under periodic review. Practitioners should track the ICO's international transfers page and DSIT announcements. If you rely on adequacy for a critical data flow, your Record of Processing Activities (ROPA) and Transfer Impact Assessments should explicitly reference the adequacy decision and include a contingency plan.

## Transfer Mechanisms

Where no adequacy decision applies, you need an appropriate safeguard under <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 46" />. The UK offers several mechanisms, each with different use cases.

### Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)

The EU's Standard Contractual Clauses, adopted by the European Commission, remain relevant for transfers from the EU. However, for transfers originating from the UK, the position is different. The UK does not directly adopt the EU's 2021 SCCs. Instead, the UK has its own instruments: the International Data Transfer Agreement (IDTA) and the UK Addendum to the EU SCCs.

### The UK International Data Transfer Agreement (IDTA)

The IDTA is a standalone contract approved by the ICO under <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 46(2)(c)">Standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commissioner</LegalCite>. It came into force on 21 March 2022 and is designed specifically for UK transfers. The IDTA is a single, modular document. You complete a series of tables covering the parties, the transfer details, the security measures, and the data subjects' rights. Unlike the EU SCCs, the IDTA does not use a module system -- it is one agreement that covers all transfer relationships (controller-to-controller, controller-to-processor, processor-to-processor, processor-to-controller).

Key features of the IDTA:

- **Mandatory clauses** that cannot be amended, ensuring a baseline level of protection.
- **Tables** that you populate with transfer-specific information (parties, data description, security requirements).
- **Flexibility** to add commercial terms, provided they do not contradict the mandatory clauses.
- **Direct enforceability** by data subjects as third-party beneficiaries.

### The UK Addendum to the EU SCCs

If you already use the EU's 2021 SCCs for EU-originating transfers, you can bolt on the UK Addendum (also called the "International Data Transfer Addendum to the EU Commission Standard Contractual Clauses") to extend coverage to UK transfers. This avoids maintaining two entirely separate agreements. The Addendum modifies the EU SCCs to reference UK law, the ICO, and UK GDPR provisions.

<Callout type="tip" title="IDTA vs UK Addendum">
Use the standalone IDTA when the transfer originates solely from the UK. Use the UK Addendum when you already have EU SCCs in place and want a single contractual package covering both EU and UK transfers. Both provide lawful safeguards -- the choice is operational, not legal.
</Callout>

### Derogations

Where neither adequacy nor an appropriate safeguard applies, <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 49">Derogations for specific situations</LegalCite> permits transfers in limited circumstances. These include:

- **Explicit consent** of the data subject, after being informed of the risks.
- **Contractual necessity** -- the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract with the data subject.
- **Important reasons of public interest** recognised in UK law.
- **Legal claims** -- the transfer is necessary for the establishment, exercise, or defence of legal claims.
- **Vital interests** of the data subject or another person.

Derogations are not intended to be used as a primary or systematic transfer mechanism. The ICO has made clear that relying on Article 49 for routine, large-scale, or repetitive transfers is unlikely to be appropriate.

<Callout type="warning" title="Derogations Are the Exception">
Do not treat Article 49 derogations as your default transfer mechanism. They are designed for occasional, non-repetitive transfers. If you find yourself relying on consent or contractual necessity for every transfer to a given country, you almost certainly need an IDTA or adequacy-based route instead.
</Callout>

## The UK-US Data Bridge

The UK Extension to the EU-US Data Privacy Framework -- commonly called the **UK-US Data Bridge** -- came into effect on 12 October 2023. It provides a streamlined adequacy-based route for transfers of personal data from the UK to US organisations that have self-certified under the Data Privacy Framework (DPF) and opted into the UK Extension.

### How It Works

The Data Bridge is not a blanket adequacy decision for the United States. It applies only to US organisations that:

1. Are eligible for the DPF (generally, organisations subject to FTC or DOT jurisdiction).
2. Have self-certified with the US Department of Commerce under the DPF.
3. Have specifically opted into the **UK Extension** of the DPF.

You can verify an organisation's certification status on the Data Privacy Framework website maintained by the US Department of Commerce. Look for the "UK Extension" designation -- a company certified under the EU-US DPF alone does not automatically qualify for UK transfers.

When a valid Data Bridge certification is in place, the transfer from the UK to that specific US organisation is treated as if it were made to an adequate country. No IDTA, no Addendum, no supplementary measures required for that particular transfer.

### Limitations

The Data Bridge has several boundaries that practitioners must understand:

- **Scope**: It covers only certified organisations. Transfers to non-certified US entities still require an IDTA or other safeguard.
- **Onward transfers**: If the certified US organisation transfers data onward to a third party (including within the US), DPF rules govern that onward transfer. You should verify the recipient's onward transfer obligations.
- **Durability**: The Data Bridge, like any adequacy-based mechanism, can be revoked. Its stability depends on continued US compliance with the commitments in Executive Order 14086 and the functioning of the Data Protection Review Court (DPRC).
- **Sector limits**: Only organisations subject to FTC or DOT enforcement jurisdiction can self-certify. This excludes banks, insurance companies, telecoms carriers, and certain other sectors.

<Callout type="info" title="Check the DPF List">
Before relying on the Data Bridge, always verify the US recipient's certification status on the official Data Privacy Framework List (dataprivacyframework.gov). Certification can lapse if a company fails to re-certify annually. A lapsed certification means the Data Bridge no longer applies, and you need an alternative safeguard.
</Callout>

## Transfer Impact Assessments

A Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) is required whenever you rely on an appropriate safeguard (such as the IDTA or UK Addendum) to transfer personal data to a country without an adequacy decision. The purpose of a TIA is to evaluate whether the legal framework in the destination country effectively protects the transferred data in practice, not just on paper.

### When a TIA Is Required

You must conduct a TIA when:

- Transferring personal data under the IDTA or UK Addendum to a non-adequate country.
- The destination country's laws or practices might undermine the protections in the transfer agreement (for example, broad government surveillance powers, weak rule of law, or limited data subject rights).

You do **not** need a TIA when transferring under an adequacy decision (including the Data Bridge) or when using Article 49 derogations.

### What a TIA Must Contain

The ICO's Transfer Risk Assessment guidance sets out the expected content. A robust TIA should cover:

1. **The transfer details**: What data, which categories of data subjects, the purpose of the transfer, the identity of the importer, and the transfer mechanism used.
2. **The legal framework of the destination country**: An assessment of relevant laws, particularly those governing government access to data, surveillance, and national security. Consider whether those laws are proportionate, subject to independent oversight, and provide effective remedies.
3. **Practical factors**: Whether the destination country's authorities actually exercise the powers identified. A law on the books is different from a law actively enforced against the type of data you transfer.
4. **Supplementary measures**: If the destination country's laws create a gap between the protection offered and the UK GDPR standard, document what technical, contractual, or organisational measures you will adopt to bridge that gap. Examples include end-to-end encryption where the importer cannot access plaintext, pseudonymisation, or split processing.
5. **Overall conclusion**: A clear determination of whether, taking all factors into account, the data will be protected to a standard essentially equivalent to that under UK GDPR.

<Callout type="tip" title="Document Your Reasoning">
A TIA is not a tick-box exercise. The ICO expects to see genuine analysis of the destination country's legal order and its practical impact on the transferred data. Record your sources, the date of your assessment, and set a review schedule. Laws change -- your TIA should be a living document.
</Callout>

### Supplementary Measures

Where a TIA identifies risks, you must implement supplementary measures sufficient to bring the level of protection up to UK GDPR standards. The EDPB's recommendations on supplementary measures (adopted and referenced by the ICO) categorise these as:

- **Technical measures**: Encryption in transit and at rest (with keys held solely by the exporter), pseudonymisation, split or multi-party processing.
- **Contractual measures**: Additional obligations on the importer to challenge disproportionate government access requests, to notify the exporter of any access demand, and to exhaust all legal avenues before disclosure.
- **Organisational measures**: Strict access controls, transparency reporting, internal policies limiting access to transferred data.

If no combination of supplementary measures can bridge the gap, you must not proceed with the transfer.

## US-Specific Considerations

The United States remains the most common destination for UK data transfers, and its legal framework warrants particular attention.

### Executive Order 14086

Executive Order 14086, signed in October 2022, introduced new safeguards for signals intelligence activities directed at non-US persons' data. It was the key instrument that enabled both the EU-US DPF and the UK-US Data Bridge. The EO establishes:

- **Necessity and proportionality principles** for US signals intelligence collection.
- **A two-tier redress mechanism**: complaints are first reviewed by the Civil Liberties Protection Officer (CLPO) within the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, and then by the Data Protection Review Court (DPRC), an independent body with binding decision-making authority.
- **Oversight requirements** for intelligence agencies, including enhanced compliance procedures.

The redress mechanism is available to UK individuals under the Data Bridge. In practice, a UK data subject who believes their data has been improperly accessed by US intelligence can lodge a complaint through the ICO, which forwards it to the US system.

### FISA Section 702 and Upstream Collection

Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) authorises the collection of foreign intelligence information from non-US persons located outside the United States. It underpins programmes such as PRISM (targeting specific selectors at providers like Google, Microsoft, and Apple) and Upstream collection (scanning internet traffic flows).

For UK practitioners, FISA 702 is the primary concern when conducting a TIA for US transfers outside the Data Bridge. Key questions include:

- Is the US data importer an **electronic communications service provider** subject to FISA 702 directives?
- Does the data being transferred fall within the categories likely to be targeted under 702 collection?
- What technical measures can prevent or limit government access (for example, client-side encryption where the provider holds no keys)?

The existence of EO 14086 mitigates some concerns, but it does not eliminate them -- particularly for high-risk data or transfers to providers known to receive FISA 702 directives.

### Adequacy Dynamics

The US adequacy picture is inherently unstable. The EU-US Safe Harbor was invalidated (Schrems I, 2015), the Privacy Shield was invalidated (Schrems II, 2020), and the current DPF faces ongoing legal challenges before the CJEU. While the UK is not directly bound by CJEU rulings post-Brexit, a successful challenge to the EU-US DPF could create political pressure on the UK-US Data Bridge and could signal weaknesses in the underlying US legal framework that the UK would need to assess independently.

Practitioners should monitor:

- CJEU proceedings regarding the EU-US DPF.
- Any changes to EO 14086 or the DPRC's functioning, particularly following changes in US administration.
- FISA 702 reauthorisation debates and any modifications to its scope or oversight mechanisms.
- The ICO's and DSIT's own assessments of the Data Bridge's continued adequacy.

## Practical Steps for Controllers

Implementing a compliant international transfer programme requires structured planning. The following steps provide a framework.

**1. Map your transfers.** Identify every data flow that crosses UK borders. Include direct transfers, onward transfers by processors, sub-processor chains, and incidental transfers (such as remote access by overseas support teams or cloud storage in non-UK regions). Record these in your ROPA.

**2. Identify your mechanism for each transfer.** For each flow, determine whether it is covered by an adequacy decision, the Data Bridge, the IDTA, the UK Addendum, or a derogation. Document the basis.

**3. Conduct TIAs where required.** For any transfer relying on the IDTA or UK Addendum, complete a Transfer Impact Assessment. Use the ICO's TIA tool or develop your own methodology, provided it covers the required elements.

**4. Implement supplementary measures.** Where a TIA identifies gaps, put technical, contractual, and organisational measures in place before the transfer begins.

**5. Build contractual scaffolding.** Execute the IDTA or UK Addendum with each data importer. Ensure the tables or annexes are fully populated -- incomplete agreements are not valid safeguards.

**6. Monitor and review.** Set review dates for your TIAs, track adequacy decision renewals, and monitor the certification status of Data Bridge recipients. Assign responsibility for ongoing monitoring to a named individual or team.

**7. Prepare fallback plans.** For critical data flows that rely on adequacy or the Data Bridge, document what alternative mechanism you will activate if the adequacy decision is revoked or a DPF certification lapses.

## Common Mistakes

Practitioners regularly encounter the same pitfalls when managing international transfers. Awareness of these can save significant time and risk.

**Assuming adequacy is permanent.** Adequacy decisions are political and legal instruments subject to change. The history of EU-US frameworks demonstrates this clearly. Never treat adequacy as a set-and-forget solution.

**Confusing EU SCCs with UK safeguards.** The EU's 2021 SCCs do not, on their own, provide a lawful safeguard for transfers from the UK. You need either the standalone IDTA or the EU SCCs plus the UK Addendum. Using EU SCCs alone for a UK transfer is non-compliant.

**Treating the Data Bridge as blanket US adequacy.** The Data Bridge applies only to DPF-certified organisations that have opted into the UK Extension. Transferring data to a non-certified US company under the assumption that "the US has adequacy" is incorrect and unlawful.

**Neglecting sub-processor chains.** Your processor may be UK-based, but their sub-processors may be in India, the Philippines, or the US. Each onward transfer in the chain requires its own lawful basis and safeguard. Ensure your processor agreements address international onward transfers explicitly.

**Conducting superficial TIAs.** A TIA that simply states "the US has rule of law" without analysing specific surveillance legislation, practical enforcement, and the nature of the data transferred will not satisfy the ICO. Invest genuine effort in the assessment.

**Relying on derogations for routine transfers.** Using explicit consent or contractual necessity as a transfer mechanism for systematic, large-scale flows to a non-adequate country is almost certainly non-compliant. Derogations under <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 49" /> are for exceptional, occasional situations.

**Failing to update the ROPA.** International transfers must be recorded in your Record of Processing Activities under <LegalCite provision="UK GDPR Art 30">Records of processing activities</LegalCite>. Many organisations record the initial transfer but fail to update the ROPA when sub-processors change or new data flows are introduced.
